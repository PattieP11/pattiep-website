# Sell First or Buy First? How to Time a Downsizing Move in Petaluma

**The short answer:** for most homeowners moving to a smaller home, selling first and then buying is the safer path, as long as there's a plan for the gap in between. Selling first tells you exactly how much money you have and makes your offer on the next home stronger. The risk is being between homes for a while, and there are good ways to handle that: a rent-back, a longer escrow, or a short stay somewhere comfortable. Buying first can work too, but only if you can comfortably carry both homes for a while. The right answer depends on your money, your timeline and how much uncertainty you can live with.

## Why is the timing so hard to get right?

Because you're doing two of the biggest transactions of your life at once, and each one depends on the other.

The money from your current home usually pays for the next one. But you can't know that number for sure until your home is under contract. Meanwhile, the home you want next won't wait forever.

Most people feel stuck right here. They worry about two things, and the two worries pull in opposite directions:

- **Getting stuck with nowhere to go:** sold the house, haven't found the next one.
- **Getting stuck with two homes:** bought the next one, the old one hasn't sold.

You can't make both risks disappear. You can choose which one you'd rather manage, and then plan for it.

## What happens if I sell first?

You put your home on the market, accept an offer, and close. Then you buy your next home with the money in hand.

**What you gain:**
- You know exactly what you have to spend.
- You're a cleaner buyer. Your offer doesn't depend on selling another home, which sellers like.
- You never carry two homes at once.

**What you risk:**
- You might need somewhere to live between closing and moving into the next home.

**How people handle the gap:**
1. **A rent-back.** You sell, and the buyer agrees to let you stay in the home for a set period after closing, paying rent. You move once, straight into the next home. Not every buyer can agree to this, so it's something to negotiate up front.
2. **A longer escrow.** Instead of a quick close, you agree on a longer one, which gives you more time to find and close on the next home.
3. **A short stay in between.** A furnished rental, family nearby, or a few weeks of storage. Not everyone's favorite, but with a plan it's a short chapter, not a crisis.

## What happens if I buy first?

You find and buy your next home, move in, then sell the old one. You only move once, and you never rush to find a place.

**What you gain:**
- You move on your own schedule.
- You can get the old home ready to sell after you've moved out, which often makes prep and showings much easier. An empty, clean home is simpler to get ready and to show.

**What you risk:**
- You may own two homes for a while and pay for both.
- If the old home takes longer to sell than you planned, that cost keeps running.

**How people pay for it:** some buy with cash. Others use financing tools their lender can explain, such as a home equity line or a bridge loan. Those are loan decisions, so talk them through with your lender before you start touring. I'll help you find one if you don't have one.

## Can I make my offer depend on selling my home?

Yes. It's called a contingent offer: you agree to buy the next home only if your current home sells.

It protects you from owning two homes. The catch is that many sellers see it as a weaker offer, because their sale now depends on yours. When a home gets several offers, a contingent one is often passed over.

It can still work, especially when the home you want has been on the market a while, or when your own home is already prepared and priced so it's likely to sell quickly. It's one more option, not a sure thing.

## Can I sell and buy at the same time?

Sometimes. With the right agreements, both closings can be lined up for the same day or a day apart, so the money from one goes straight into the other.

It takes planning and cooperation from both sides, and a little give in the timeline in case one deal slips. When it works, it's the smoothest version of the move. When it doesn't, you need a backup plan, usually one of the gap options above.

## How do I decide which is right for me?

Start with three questions:

1. **Could you comfortably pay for both homes for a few months?** If not, selling first is usually safer.
2. **How would you feel living somewhere temporary for a few weeks?** If that sounds miserable, plan for a rent-back or a longer escrow from the start.
3. **How ready is your current home?** A home that's inspected, planned and prepped before it goes on the market gives you more control over timing, because there are fewer surprises once a buyer is involved.

That third question is why I start every sale the same way. Before your home goes on the market, I get a pre-listing inspection, build a plan for the repairs and updates worth doing, and set a timeline for the prep and the launch date. When you know your home's condition and your launch date, you can plan the purchase around it instead of guessing.

## What mistakes do people make with timing?

- **Shopping before knowing their numbers.** Falling in love with a home before you know what your current home will bring, and what you'll keep, leads to painful choices later.
- **Listing a home that isn't ready.** Surprises found by a buyer's inspector can stretch your escrow or reopen your price, and suddenly your timing is off.
- **Assuming a rent-back is automatic.** It has to be agreed to with the buyer. Ask for it in your plan from day one.
- **Skipping the lender conversation.** If there's any chance you'll buy first, talk to a lender early so you know what's possible.
- **Forgetting the tax side.** Selling a home you've owned for a long time can have tax effects, and California has rules that may let some homeowners keep a lower property tax base when they move. Talk to your CPA or tax advisor before you list. I'm not a tax advisor, and the answer depends on your situation.
- **Trying to hold it all in your head.** Two transactions mean two sets of deadlines. Write them on one calendar.

## What does this look like in real life?

Here are two ways it can go. These are examples to show the process, not real clients.

**Example one: sell first, with a rent-back.** A couple wants to move from a two-story home to a single-level one across town. They don't want to carry two homes. We prepare their home and list it, and in the offer we ask the buyer for a rent-back. With the sale locked in, they shop with a firm budget, write a clean offer on the single-level home, and move once, straight from the old house to the new one.

**Example two: buy first, then sell an empty house.** An owner can comfortably cover both homes for a few months. They buy the next home first and move in. The old house is then empty, so the repairs, paint and cleaning go quickly, and showings are easy. It goes on the market ready.

Both work. They fit different people.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is it better to sell first or buy first when downsizing?**
For most people, selling first is safer, because you know your budget and you never carry two homes. Buying first works when you can comfortably pay for both homes for a while.

**What is a rent-back?**
An agreement where you sell your home and then stay in it for a set period after closing, paying rent to the buyer. It lets you move once. The buyer has to agree to it.

**What is a contingent offer?**
An offer to buy a home that only goes through if your current home sells. It protects you, but many sellers see it as weaker, especially when there are several offers.

**Can I close on both homes on the same day?**
Sometimes. It takes planning and cooperation on both sides, plus a backup plan in case one closing slips.

**Do I need to talk to a lender if I'm paying cash?**
It's still worth it if there's any chance you'll buy before you sell. A lender can tell you what options you'd have.

**Will I owe taxes when I sell?**
It depends on your situation. Talk to your CPA or tax advisor before you list.
