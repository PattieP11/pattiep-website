Pre-Listing Inspections in Petaluma: Why I Inspect Before Your Home Goes on the Market

By Pattie Philibosian, REALTOR® · eXp Realty of Northern California, Inc. · October 27, 2026

The short answer: a pre-listing inspection is a professional home inspection you order before your home goes on the market, instead of waiting for the buyer's inspector to find things during escrow. It costs you an inspection fee up front. In return, you learn what's wrong while you still have time to choose what to fix, get fair bids and plan your price, and buyers see a home with fewer surprises. I get one on every home I list.

Pattie Philibosian is a REALTOR® with eXp Realty of Northern California helping buyers and sellers in Sonoma and Petaluma. The pre-listing inspection is step two of her Committed Partner Plan, and here's why it comes so early.

What is a pre-listing inspection?

It's the same kind of inspection a buyer orders after their offer is accepted, just done earlier, by you. A licensed home inspector goes through the house from the roof to the foundation and writes up what they find: the roof, plumbing, electrical, heating and cooling, windows, doors, drainage, and signs of water or damage.

In California, sellers often order a separate termite and pest inspection too, since wood damage is its own specialty.

The report doesn't pass or fail your home. It's a list of conditions, from small fixes to bigger items, so you can make decisions with real information.

Why not just wait for the buyer's inspection?

Because by then, you've lost your time and your bargaining power.

When a buyer's inspector finds something during escrow, you're working against a contingency deadline. The buyer may ask for repairs, a credit or a lower price, and you're deciding fast, often with one contractor's quote and no time to compare.

When you find it first:

  • You choose. Fix it, price for it, or disclose it and leave it. Your call, on your schedule.
  • You get fair bids. Time to compare contractors instead of taking the first number.
  • You price with confidence. No guessing about what's lurking.
  • You avoid the late surprise. Less chance the deal falls apart or the price reopens after the buyer's inspection.

Do I have to share the report with buyers?

Plan on it. In California, sellers have to disclose what they know about a home's condition. Once you have an inspection report, what it found is something you know.

That sounds like a downside. In practice, it's one of the biggest benefits. When buyers and their agents can read the report before they write an offer, they know what they're getting. Offers come in with the condition already understood, instead of being renegotiated a week into escrow.

The disclosure forms and your agent will walk you through exactly what needs to be shared. If you have a legal question about disclosure, talk to a real estate attorney.

Will the buyer still do their own inspection?

Usually, yes, and they should. Buyers have the right to their own inspections. A pre-listing report doesn't replace theirs. It means fewer surprises when they do it, because the big items are already known and either fixed, priced in or disclosed.

What happens after the report comes back?

This is where the real work starts. I go through the report with you and sort every item into a plan:

  1. Fix it. Safety items, anything likely to worry a buyer's lender, and things that are cheap to fix and costly to leave.
  2. Refresh it. Paint, lighting, hardware, a deep clean, the yard. These shape how a home feels in photos and showings.
  3. Disclose and leave it. Some items aren't worth fixing before a sale. They get disclosed, and the price reflects them.

Then I build a written timeline for the repairs, the prep and the date the home goes on the market. I line up and manage the vendors, and I'm hands-on with the details: picking paint, buying hardware, swapping light bulbs, whatever it takes to get the home ready.

What does it cost, and is it worth it?

The inspection fee depends on the size and age of the home and the inspector. Ask two or three inspectors for a quote before you book.

Whether it's worth it comes down to one question: would you rather learn about a problem now, when you have time and choices, or in the middle of escrow, when you have neither? For most sellers, a pre-listing inspection is one of the smallest costs in the whole sale, and it protects the biggest number.

What mistakes do sellers make with inspections?

  • Skipping it to save the fee, then giving up far more in a rushed repair credit later.
  • Fixing everything on the list. Not every item is worth your money. Sort first, then spend.
  • Hiding what the report found. It has to be disclosed, and trying to bury it can cost you a buyer's trust, or worse.
  • Using a contractor's guess instead of a real bid. When an item matters, get it priced properly.
  • Getting the report and doing nothing with it. The value is in the plan you build from it.

What does this look like in real life?

Here's how it can go. This is an example to show the process, not a real client.

A Petaluma owner is getting ready to sell a home they've lived in for many years. The pre-listing inspection finds a slow leak under a bathroom sink, a few electrical outlets that need attention, and an older water heater. It also notes worn paint and some dated light fixtures. The plan: fix the leak and the outlets, disclose the water heater's age, repaint two rooms, and replace the light fixtures. Six weeks later, the home goes on the market with the report available to buyers. The offers that come in already account for the water heater, and the buyer's own inspection finds no surprises.
